# Environment research: the Khuzestan front, September 1980

Reference notes for the procedural battlefield. Every point is paraphrased and carries the source it came from.
Points marked **(verify)** are inferences, general knowledge or things the sources do not state directly; treat
them as placeholders until a better source or a reference photo confirms them. Where a feature has no research
support it should be drawn generically.

Scope: the opening weeks of the war (22 September 1980 onward) on the southern front: Khorramshahr, Abadan,
Ahvaz, Susangerd, Dezful, the Karun and Shatt al-Arab (Arvand Rud), and the Hawizeh (Hoveyzeh) marshes.
Some later-war features (1981 and after) are listed separately because they are useful but not period-exact.

---

## 1. Climate and light in late September

- Ahvaz in September is very hot and almost cloudless: daily highs fall from roughly 44 °C to 39 °C over the
  month, lows from about 28 °C to 23 °C, rain is close to nil, and skies are clear nearly all of the time.
  Winds come mostly from the west. Source: https://weatherspark.com/m/104596/9/Average-Weather-in-September-in-Ahvaz-Iran
  - Art consequence: hard, high-contrast midday light, pale bleached ground, no puddles from rain in September.
    Standing water comes from rivers, canals, irrigation and deliberate flooding, not from weather.
- The first rains come later in autumn. By January 1981 the plain near Susangerd had turned to deep mud after
  rain and seasonal flooding, and armour that left the paved road bogged down.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Nasr
  - Art consequence: a "wet season" variant is legitimate for later scenarios; for September keep mud to
    riverbanks, canal edges, marsh margins and flooded zones.
- Dust storms (shamal) carry dust from the Tigris-Euphrates plain south-east over Khuzestan and the Gulf on
  strong north-westerly winds. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/dust-over-the-persian-gulf-89705
  (verify: the page redirected during research, so the wording was not read directly; the north-west direction of
  the shamal is widely documented but should be confirmed).
- Desert dust sources inside Khuzestan are saline, calcareous flats. Source:
  https://old.oiccpress.com/journal-of-rangeland-science/article/species-diversity-of-desert-and-relationship-to-soil-properties-in-dust-sources-of-khuzestan-southwest-of-iran/
- Sun position at the autumn equinox (about 31° N): sunrise and sunset close to due east and due west, noon sun
  about 59° above the southern horizon. (verify: computed from latitude, not from a source.)
  - Design decision: the game map is drawn north-up, so the true noon sun would light from the bottom of the
    screen, which makes relief read inside-out (craters look like mounds). Noon lighting therefore uses the
    standard upper-left light; dawn and dusk use true east and west.

## 2. Ground: soils, salt and colour

- The Khuzestan plain is alluvial, and its farmland suffers from soil salinity and sodicity; the dominant soil
  factors measured in desert dust sources were carbonate (CaCO3), salinity (EC), sodium (Na, SAR, ESP) and low
  organic carbon. Sources: https://lmj.areeo.ac.ir/article_134014.html?lang=en and
  https://old.oiccpress.com/journal-of-rangeland-science/article/species-diversity-of-desert-and-relationship-to-soil-properties-in-dust-sources-of-khuzestan-southwest-of-iran/
  - Art consequence: ground colours should be pale buff, grey-brown and silty, not the orange of dune deserts.
    Salt shows as whitish crust on low, poorly drained ground. (verify: exact colours need reference photos.)
- Abadan island lies mostly no more than two metres above mean sea level, so the land is very flat, and low land
  near the coast is more saline and more often flooded by salty water. Source:
  https://www.flevolandsgeheugen.nl/page/12901/polders-for-date-palms-at-abadan-isle-in-iran
- The Karun carries a heavy silt load into the Shatt al-Arab, enough that the channel needs constant dredging.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatt_al-Arab
  - Art consequence: river and canal water is brown-olive and opaque, with lighter silty edges, not blue.
- Sand dunes exist in parts of Khuzestan but most of the battle area is flat silt plain. (verify: no direct source
  found on dune fields near the 1980 battle area; keep dunes low and sparse.)

## 3. Vegetation

- Date palms (Phoenix dactylifera) line the Shatt al-Arab and its branches. In the mid-1970s the Shatt al-Arab
  region held around 17 to 18 million palms, roughly a fifth of all date palms in the world; war, salt and pests
  later destroyed most of them (about 5 million lost on the Iranian side). Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatt_al-Arab
  - Art consequence: along the waterways, palm groves are dense and continuous, not isolated clumps.
- On Abadan island the palm gardens were irrigated by the tide: the Gulf tide pushed river water up canals dug
  roughly at right angles to the river, flooding the plots at high tide. Canals were wider and deeper downstream.
  Dikes protected low land, and farmsteads often sat on raised mounds. Source:
  https://www.flevolandsgeheugen.nl/page/12901/polders-for-date-palms-at-abadan-isle-in-iran
  - Art consequence: palm gardens are cut by a comb of narrow canals running perpendicular to the river, with
    low dikes; houses sit on slightly raised ground.
- Orchard layout: palms planted in rows at around 8 to 10 m spacing. (verify: general date-cultivation practice,
  no Khuzestan-specific source found; older gardens are probably less regular.)
- September is date harvest season, so ripe clusters (yellow, orange or brown) hang below the crowns. (verify)
- Native riverbank woodland in Khuzestan is dominated by Euphrates poplar (Populus euphratica), studied along the
  Karun and Dez. Source: https://journals.areeo.ac.ir/article_115185.html
- In the wider Mesopotamian marshes, riverbanks and islands carry poplar, tamarisk and willow. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamian_Marshes
- Desert flats are covered by low salt-tolerant shrubs: Seidlitzia rosmarinus, Halocnemum strobilaceum, Tamarix
  species, Aeluropus lagopoides (a low grass), Atriplex, Salsola and Cornulaca. Source:
  https://old.oiccpress.com/journal-of-rangeland-science/article/species-diversity-of-desert-and-relationship-to-soil-properties-in-dust-sources-of-khuzestan-southwest-of-iran/
  - Art consequence: scrub is sparse, small, grey-green to dull olive, often in clumps on low hummocks; low grass
    patches; tamarisk as larger feathery grey-green shrubs. Nothing lush outside irrigated land.
- Marsh vegetation: reeds (Phragmites australis), cattail (Typha domingensis) and papyrus sedge. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamian_Marshes

## 4. Water: rivers, canals, marshes and floods

- The Shatt al-Arab is about 230 m wide at Basra and widens to about 800 m at its mouth. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatt_al-Arab
- Opposite Abadan in October 1980 the river was described as roughly 350 m wide and fast-flowing. Source:
  https://www.csmonitor.com/1980/1006/100645.html
- The Hawizeh marshes are fed by branches of the Tigris and by the Karkheh from the Iranian side, and they drain
  onward rather than forming a closed salt basin. The marsh people build houses from reeds. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawizeh_Marshes
- Marsh villages are often reachable only by boat, and the marsh people herd water buffalo. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamian_Marshes
- Reed guest houses (mudhif) are long tunnel-like halls of bundled reed arches covered with reed mats. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudhif (verify: the article is about southern Iraq; reed buildings on the Iranian
  side of the Hawizeh are likely but not confirmed by this source.)
- Iran used flooding as a defence: during the Khorramshahr battle Iranian forces flooded marsh areas around the
  city so that Iraqi armour had to move along narrow strips of dry land. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War
  - Art consequence: flooded fields with sheet water, drowned crop rows and palm trunks standing in water, and
    raised roads or dikes as the only dry routes.
- Iraq built dams, dykes, sluices and canals to drain parts of the Hawizeh marsh during the 1980s war for military
  advantage. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamian_Marshes (later than September 1980.)
- Large modern irrigation schemes (the Dez dam and irrigation project) existed in northern Khuzestan before the
  war, with concrete-lined canal networks and sugar cane estates. (verify: sources found only by title,
  e.g. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dez_Dam and
  https://associationforiranianstudies.org/content/eroding-communities-water-development-modern-irrigation-khuzestan;
  not read in detail.)

## 5. Towns, villages and buildings

- Khorramshahr in 1980 had narrow, tight streets in the centre (too tight for Chieftain tanks to work well),
  taller buildings used as observation posts, a port, a railway station, a slaughterhouse, barracks, a government
  building and a Grand Mosque. Dikes ringed the outskirts. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Khorramshahr_(1980)
- Abadan grew as an oil company town: the refinery formed a large metal barrier across the middle of the
  island; British staff lived in garden bungalows (Braim), company workers in planned districts such as Bawarda
  and Bahmanshir, and crowded informal housing grew at the other end. The town had cinemas, clubs, schools and
  squares, and many buildings used imported red brick. Source:
  https://ajammc.com/2015/02/18/abadan-capital-of-the-world/
- Company architecture in Abadan used British brick facing, terrazzo and garden-suburb layouts; some Bawarda
  housing was built in a Dutch style. Source:
  https://round-city.com/the-oil-and-the-brick-tales-of-a-scotsman-in-persia/
- In December 1980 Abadan showed shell damage to hospitals, the police headquarters, the museum and homes near
  the refinery; a cinema was burnt out; shop windows were covered with foil; streets in the centre were still
  clean. Source: https://csmonitor.com/1980/1215/121532.html
- Dezful is known as the "city of bricks", with decorative patterned brickwork on facades and cornices, cool
  underground rooms (shavadan) near the river, and a congregational mosque with a courtyard, brick dome and twin
  minarets. Sources:
  https://www.tehrantimes.com/news/527902/Jameh-Mosque-of-Dezful-An-architectural-treasure-in-Iran-s-city and
  https://jias.kashanu.ac.ir/article_111758_en.html
- Typical houses in towns: flat roofs with low parapets used for sleeping in summer, inward-facing courtyards,
  blank walls to the street, fired brick or concrete block in towns and mud brick in villages. (verify: general
  knowledge of the region; no source found that describes Khuzestan houses this way. Reference photos needed.)
- Rooftop clutter in 1980: water tanks, TV antennas, laundry lines, evaporative coolers. (verify)
- Village layout: loose clusters of courtyard houses with mud-brick walls, animal pens and reed or palm-frond
  shelters. (verify)

## 6. Oil infrastructure

- Abadan refinery had a capacity of about 635,000 barrels per day in 1980, with petrochemical plants attached,
  and was largely destroyed by Iraq early in the invasion. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan_Refinery
- In the first days of the war the refinery was hit and set on fire. Source:
  https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19800925.2.8 (headline: "Huge oil refinery in flames as
  Iraqis win war's first round"; article body not read.)
- In October 1980 the refinery ruins were still smouldering, with occasional explosions, and a pall of smoke hung
  for miles. Source: https://www.csmonitor.com/1980/1006/100645.html
- In December 1980 the Bawarda tank farm in south-east Abadan had blackened, partly melted storage tanks, and the
  refinery fire lit up the city at night. Source: https://csmonitor.com/1980/1215/121532.html
- The Ahvaz oil field is about 67 km long and 6 km wide, discovered in 1953 and producing since 1954.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahvaz_Field
- Gas flaring at Khuzestan oil fields is a long-standing feature (sources describe present-day flaring).
  Source: https://iranwire.com/en/features/138161-khuzestans-silent-crisis-oil-wealth-shrouded-by-poisoned-air/
  (verify: flare stacks in 1980 are very likely but the source is modern.)
- The oil company also ran railways in the region. Source:
  https://rogerfarnworth.com/2020/11/19/railways-in-iran-part-11-anglo-persian-oil-company-ltd/ (not read in
  detail.)
- Producing fields in 1980 would mostly show wellheads, flow lines, separator stations and pumping stations rather
  than standing drilling derricks; derricks appear only where wells were being drilled. (verify)
- Storage tanks: large cylindrical tanks in bunded (earth-walled) tank farms; crude tanks often floating-roof.
  (verify: general refinery practice, not confirmed for Abadan in 1980.)
- Pipelines run between Ahvaz, Abadan and the export terminals; many run above ground on low supports.
  (verify: a pipeline factsheet was found by title only: https://gem.wiki/Ahwaz%E2%80%93Abadan_Oil_Pipelines)

## 7. Military terrain of the war

### September to December 1980 (period-exact)

- Iranian defenders built a series of dikes on the outskirts of Khorramshahr: the first line held infantry, the
  second tanks, artillery and anti-tank weapons. Iraq had cleared most of them by 30 September. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Khorramshahr_(1980)
- Iraqi infantry dug foxholes and trenches into the shady gardens opposite the Abadan refinery. Source:
  https://www.csmonitor.com/1980/1006/100645.html
- Iranian troops dug trenches and tank positions facing north toward the Abadan to Bandar Mashar road, and lined
  the Shatt al-Arab to stop crossings. Vehicles were caked with dried mud as camouflage. Source:
  https://csmonitor.com/1980/1215/121532.html
- Iraqi forces crossed the Bahmanshir river on a pontoon bridge, and crossed the Karun about 4 km upstream of the
  Shatt al-Arab in early October. Sources: https://csmonitor.com/1980/1215/121532.html and
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Abadan
- The bridge linking Khorramshahr to Abadan was a key objective. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Khorramshahr_(1980)
- Burning Iraqi tanks were left near Susangerd after fighting in November 1980. Source:
  https://www.csmonitor.com/1980/1124/112420.html
- Iran crossed the Karkheh on pontoon bridges in January 1981; the bridges were bombed and rebuilt. Iraqi tanks
  were dug into the mud in hull-down positions in a three-sided ambush. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Nasr
- The Ahvaz White Bridge (Pol Sefid) over the Karun, completed in 1936, is about 500 m long, concrete and metal,
  two lanes. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pol_Sefid_(Ahvaz) (verify: sources disagree on whether it is
  an arch or a suspension bridge.)

### 1981 and later (useful, not September 1980)

- Iraqi engineers built earthen revetments, trenches and machine-gun nests, and moved to a three-line defence
  with barbed wire, minefields and bunkers; tanks were entrenched in static positions. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War
- Around occupied Khorramshahr (1981 to 1982) Iraq built three belts of minefields and earthworks, planted metal
  spikes against helicopter landings, and stood hundreds of derelict cars upright as obstacles. Source:
  https://kavehfarrokh.com/military-history-1900-present/the-liberation-of-khorramshahr-may-24-25-1981/
- Marshes absorbed artillery and negated armour in the 1984 fighting; Iran used pontoon bridges across rivers and
  wetlands. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War
- Khuzestan was left with very large mine contamination (an initial estimate of about 15,000 km2), including
  anti-personnel and anti-vehicle mines, minefield barriers, mines in marshes and mines displaced by floods.
  Source: https://www.mineactionreview.org/assets/downloads/Iran_Clearing_the_Mines_2024.pdf
  - Art consequence: minefield markers are legitimate but should be sparse and improvised in 1980 (verify:
    marking practice in 1980 is undocumented here).
